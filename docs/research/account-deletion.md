# Account deletion on a community and catalogue platform

Research date: 27 October 2026. This report is evidence and a proposed policy, not an adopted contract or an implementation change.

## Recommendation

Keep REZICS's irreversible deletion barrier and minimal attribution tombstone. Offer an export and clearly explain the fate of contributions before confirmation. Do not introduce a mandatory cooling-off period: the sources support several policies, rather than a shared 30-day rule. Disable authority immediately; finish physical erasure through durable, monitored cleanup. Keep useful public discussions and catalogue history with a generic deleted-account attribution, subject to separate removal of personal information and a documented lawful basis for retention.

Add an explicit organization-closing procedure, a contribution-erasure policy, and a purpose-specific retention schedule. Resolve operated accounts before deleting their operator. Deleting an organization must not delete its members' independent personal accounts. Retain consent rows after Auth erasure only as inert, time-bounded cleanup data, never as continuing authorization.

These recommendations combine the patterns below; they are product choices except where the legal section identifies a requirement. In particular, a stable account ID, retained text and historical relationships are not automatically anonymous just because the profile name is gone.

## Method and evidence boundaries

Official product documentation, the GDPR, EDPB material and pinned upstream source were inspected. No reference repository was installed, built or executed. Shallow reference clones are under `.temp/account-deletion-refs/{mastodon,discourse,keycloak,hydra,zitadel}/`. The task's suggested external clone directory conflicts with its worktree-only write restriction; this report follows the write restriction and AGENTS.md's `.temp/` rule.

REZICS was examined at base commit `b83e8ec43c3056755cc6593b35dafa2e52f2e7f0`. Local links below refer to that design as inspected. The consent cleanup rule supplied with the research question is treated as intended companion work, not as an already implemented sweeper. Absence of a documented product guarantee is reported as such, rather than filled in from third-party answers.

### Organizations and automated accounts

GitHub permits organization owners to delete the organization, warns about owned resources and recommends backup; its instructions do not require removing every member first. A person's deletion requires resolving organization ownership or membership first. Organization membership does not make the members' personal accounts disposable organization data. GitHub distinguishes organizations, personal accounts and customer-managed machine users; the inspected documentation does not establish an automatic machine-account cascade when an operator leaves. See [account types](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/learning-about-github/types-of-github-accounts) and the deletion sources above.

ZITADEL uses a different tenant boundary: [organization deletion](https://zitadel.com/docs/reference/api/org/zitadel.org.v2.OrganizationService.DeleteOrganization) deletes its users, projects and grants. This is evidence for explicitly documenting ownership and cascade boundaries, not a suitable default for deleting independent REZICS members. The sources do not share a universal rule for bots or organizations. The common concern is avoiding orphaned authority and surprising deletion of somebody else's resources.

## Patterns the sources share

1. Account access, public contributions, and private identity records have separate lifetimes. A successful logout is not complete erasure; a surviving public contribution need not preserve a live identity.
2. Export precedes irreversible loss of access. Export availability is common, but its scope varies and its preparation time is not a cancellation period.
3. There is no common cooling-off duration: X has 30 days, several services make deletion irreversible immediately, and Google describes recovery without one universal account-help deadline.
4. Community/history systems often preserve contributions with replacement attribution; personal publishing systems can instead remove them. Neither approach establishes a universal rule for reviews or catalogue edits.
5. Ownership and delegation need explicit resolution. An organization/tenant boundary determines whether users are independent members or owned identities. Bots require an operator policy, not an inferred identity-provider cascade.
6. Revocation must cover sessions, refresh capability and all grant types. Audit/event retention and remote copies need separate rules. A generic deleted-user label is only one layer of privacy work.

## Law versus product policy

The legal authority is the [EU GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj). For accessible article text, the links below use the UK National Archives' official mirror of the **original adopted EU text**, not subsequent UK amendments. EDPB guidance supplies EU supervisory interpretation. Applicability depends on the processing and jurisdiction; these sources do not create a worldwide account-deletion product specification.

- **Erasure grounds and exceptions:** [Article 17](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/17/adopted) requires erasure without undue delay when a ground applies, including no remaining need, withdrawal without another basis, qualifying objection or unlawful processing. Necessary expression/information, legal obligations, specified public interests, qualifying archiving/research and legal claims can justify exceptions. There is no unconditional right to delete everybody else's discussion or all historical facts, and no blanket exception for community content either.
- **Handling the request:** [Article 12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/12/adopted) requires action/information without undue delay, normally within one month; a justified extension of up to two further months must be explained within the first month. This response framework is not an automatic three-month storage allowance. Identity checks must be proportionate. Refusal requires reasons and information about remedies.
- **Export:** [Article 20](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/20/adopted) provides portability for qualifying automated processing based on consent or contract, covering data provided by the subject in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format, with others' rights protected. It is narrower than exporting every internal record. Offer a useful archive as product policy too; do not condition erasure on accepting or waiting for it.
- **Other copies:** Article 17(2) requires reasonable steps concerning publicly distributed personal data; [Article 19](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/19/adopted) addresses recipient notification, subject to its exceptions. Neither promises removal of every independent copy. Internal replicas, search indexes and processors are part of the controller's erasure plan, not independent public copies to ignore.
- **Abuse and holds:** [Articles 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/5/adopted) and [6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/6/adopted) require minimization, storage limitation and a lawful basis. Security/fraud needs require necessity and an appropriate basis; “abuse prevention” alone is not an Article 17 exception. A legal claim or specific retention obligation can support a limited hold, not retaining unrelated passwords or active authorization indefinitely.

The EDPB's [individual-rights guide](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/sme/be-compliant/respect-individuals-rights_en) explains erasure, portability and exceptions. Its [2025 coordinated enforcement report, adopted 10 February 2026](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-02/edpb_cef-report_2025_right-to-erasure_en.pdf), makes the operational distinction especially clear:

- Section 4.2.1, pages 13–14: closing an account is not necessarily erasing personal data; controllers need to locate data across their systems.
- Sections 4.2.4–4.2.5, pages 17–20: exceptions need individual justification and retention must relate to purpose; applying the longest period to everything is problematic.
- Section 4.2.6, pages 20–21: backups are within scope, with verifiable arrangements rather than a blanket exclusion.
- Section 4.2.7, page 22: ineffective anonymization or mere pseudonymization does not satisfy erasure.

Consequently, REZICS needs a retention register with data class, purpose/basis, trigger, maximum duration, deletion mechanism and hold review/release. No cited rule sets one universal log-retention duration or mandates a 30-day cooling-off window. A legal person's organization closure is primarily a product/contract matter; natural persons' data inside that organization still needs privacy handling. Do not make ownership handoff an indefinite barrier to a person's statutory erasure request: provide an assisted resolution path.

## Comparison with REZICS and proposed changes

### 1. Cooling-off and cancellation: matches an established pattern

[Access's account lifecycle](../../services/kernel/src/access/README.md) makes deletion terminal; it never reinstates a deleted account. This matches immediate irreversible closure in several sources, though not X's recovery model. Recommend retaining **no cancellation window after the barrier**, with fresh confirmation and explicit consequences before it. A future reversible deactivation feature would be a separate state, not restoration of a deleted identity.

The committed continuation's expiry is the earlier of 300 seconds after barrier time and approval expiry minus its protected budget. It limits cleanup authority, not the person's right to cancel and not the time allowed to erase data. Expiry does not release holds; governance can renew it. Keep that safety property, but add durable retry ownership, expiry alerts and authorized renewal/escalation so an Auth outage cannot leave cleanup pending indefinitely. The research does not find a reason to enlarge a security capability's lifetime into a recovery window.

### 2. Export first: a policy gap in the inspected deletion contract

The inspected Access deletion sections and Auth `eraseAccount` do not establish a user export step or an archive-completion prerequisite. Do not infer an existing complete export from unrelated APIs. This falls short of the common product pattern and needs explicit portability coverage.

Recommend an optional pre-deletion archive: profile, own posts/reviews, catalogue contributions with relevant history, preferences and appropriate private account data, in JSON/CSV with media where applicable. Scope organization export to authorized organization resources and exclude other members' unrelated private data. Exclude credentials, bearer secrets and unrestricted internal abuse records. Explain third-party data redactions. Use authenticated, expiring delivery and a published preparation bound; offer immediate deletion independently of export readiness.

### 3. Public content and attribution: partial match, incomplete erasure policy

Access's public-identity projection retains `id`, `state` and `kind` for a deleted account while withholding its former handle, display name, profile and operator. [The tombstone SQL](../../services/kernel/src/access/lifecycle-fence.sql) scrubs profile fields, but history, grants, assignments, handles and coordination facts are intentionally retained. This supports the ghost/renamed-contributor pattern; it does not by itself decide what happens to posts, reviews or revision bodies.

Recommend adopting these separate content rules:

| Data                                 | Proposed public result                                                                           | Additional handling                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Profile and author cards             | Generic “Deleted account” or “Deleted organization”; no old name, handle, avatar or profile link | Clear cached cards, search results and other derived identity labels. A deleted actor must not be labelled a human: the projection no longer distinguishes previously automated actors.                                                                                    |
| Posts and reviews                    | Preserve discussion/review value under the generic attribution by default                        | Offer content deletion before closure and an accessible privacy/removal channel afterwards. Assess identifying free text, attachments, quotations and revision history; a profile scrub cannot erase them. Document the retention basis and applicable exceptions.         |
| Catalogue facts and edit history     | Keep useful factual edits and a minimal history/attribution record                               | Remove unnecessary personal metadata and redact personal information in descriptions, edit summaries and old revisions. Check the actual contribution licence before choosing the minimum surviving attribution; Wikipedia's licence policy is not automatically REZICS's. |
| Private profile data and preferences | No public retention                                                                              | Erase when no longer needed; separate any justified, access-restricted evidence hold.                                                                                                                                                                                      |

Call the result **de-identified attribution**, not guaranteed anonymous data. A stable ID can link retained content to screenshots, citations or internal records. Kind-only public attribution matches the shared identity/content separation; declaring that it alone completes GDPR erasure would not. Historical operator/handle links need restricted visibility, a documented purpose and their own retention review, rather than indefinite retention by default.

### 4. Organizations, members and bots: bot policy matches; organization closure needs a path

Access permits personal self-deletion and automated self-deletion or deletion by the current operator's managers. It blocks operator deletion until each operated account has transferred or been deleted and its responsibility released. This is a sound explicit resolution policy where the compared products do not supply one common cascade rule. Keep it; expose the blockers and offer transfer or delete, including revocation/rotation of the bot's sessions and credentials as appropriate. Never silently orphan a bot or transfer its authority to the platform.

There is a concrete closure gap in [the lifecycle implementation](../../services/kernel/src/access/lifecycle.ts): `setMembership` prevents removing/demoting the last owner, while `tombstone` requires an organization to have **zero owners**. Ordinary owner-operated organizations cannot reach deletion through those two commands alone. The zero-owner case exercised by [the owner tests](../../services/kernel/test/access/owner-cost.test.ts) does not supply that missing workflow. The tombstone check is not a requirement that all ordinary members already be absent.

Recommend a dedicated owner-authorized organization-close protocol that terminalizes organization authority/memberships as part of closure without permitting a live ownerless organization. Resolve operated accounts first, notify members, dispose of organization-owned resources explicitly, and preserve each member's independent personal account. Retained membership history must confer no authority once the organization is deleted. Add an assisted route for inaccessible owners and privacy requests. This follows GitHub's shared-membership boundary rather than ZITADEL's tenant-owned-user cascade; it is a recommendation for adoption, not a code change in this report.

### 5. Sessions, consents, credentials and logs: good authority barrier; physical erasure needs a complete inventory

[Auth's `eraseAccount`](../../services/auth/src/service.ts) requires Access to report the account deleted and fenced, then deletes the Auth `user` row. [The workflow](../../services/auth/src/workflows.ts) tombstones before Auth erasure, then releases responsibility and redirects the old handle. Current Access checks reject new person-initiated work for deleted/fenced accounts; committed cleanup uses separately bounded authority. This matches immediate authority removal followed by idempotent cleanup, including during Auth failure.

[The Auth schema](../../services/auth/db/schema.sql) cascades the user deletion into credential accounts, sessions, passkeys, public-key/assertion records, recovery/opening records and user-linked OAuth access/refresh rows. This matches the IdP cleanup pattern. It also cascades owned OAuth clients, potentially affecting other people's grants to those clients: describe that boundary and require transfer/re-registration where continued shared client service is intended. Verify application sessions and externally issued/federated credentials separately; deleting local rows cannot revoke an upstream provider account.

`auth_consent` has a client foreign key but no user foreign key. The supplied companion rule erases those rows once the access-token lifetime has passed after Auth erasure. **This is compatible as delayed physical cleanup, not a common OAuth requirement.** It is safe only if tombstoning immediately prevents every issuance/refresh/use path from deriving authority from surviving consent. Test client/subject combinations and grants without surviving consent-flow records, the distinction highlighted by Hydra's implementations.

Record Auth erasure time and the last possible expiry of already issued access tokens, including verifier leeway; use a bounded deadline rather than an unbounded dependence on future configuration changes. The cleanup cutoff must cover all relevant token lifetimes, not assume every grant uses the same one. Missing consent must fail closed. In REZICS, the live Access state check can deny even an unexpired self-contained JWT immediately; every relying party must either enforce that check or have an explicitly bounded residual-token risk. Do not retain refresh capability until the consent cleanup deadline.

The user-row cascade is not a full data inventory. [Auth's storage contract](../../services/auth/README.md) describes workflow staging/request and effect receipts without a user foreign key, and verification values can contain account identifiers. Access retains historical coordination facts too. Inspect those payloads, event/audit logs, IP addresses, error telemetry, caches, exports, replicas and backups. Set deletion/anonymization triggers for each; do not infer cascade erasure from a column's name or from “deleted” state. ZITADEL's surviving events and Discourse's optional identity logging show why this matters.

For timing, propose: remove ordinary identity/security material during Auth cleanup; purge inert consents at the bounded token cutoff; scrub unnecessary identity fields in operational receipts/logs during cleanup; retain only specifically justified security/incident/legal evidence under restricted access until its scheduled expiry or hold release. Publish backup expiry and prevent restored backups from resurrecting deleted identities. Fixed durations for incident evidence depend on the actual purpose and applicable obligations, not on the 300-second capability or a borrowed vendor's backup duration.

### 6. Statutory erasure, abuse and legal holds: policy still required

The fencing and continuation machinery supports controlled erasure, but neither an irreversible state nor a database cascade demonstrates compliance across every store. The inspected sections do not supply a comprehensive retention/exception matrix, post-closure request channel or recipient-notification process. Indefinite history and receipt retention need explicit justification where they remain personal data.

Recommend one closure result shown to the person: immediately disabled identity; categories erased; contributions retained with replacement attribution; any narrowly withheld categories, reason and review/expiry; and how to exercise privacy rights after losing login. Keep a minimal erasure/hold receipt without unnecessarily recording the erased values. Separate product closure from a statutory request so that missing exports, last-owner disputes, suspension or bot handoff cannot silently postpone the legal response. Review abuse retention for necessity and proportionality, and release holds when their purpose ends.
